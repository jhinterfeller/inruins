The Seven Seas

Budd Inlet

The Adventuress

Their breath floated up like pipe smoke through the crisp air. No one noticed the chill this morning. The pulses were quick, full of excitement. The mission was to secure a bowhead whale for the American Museum of Natural History. It was February 1913, and the 133-foot schooner Adventuress was ready for her maiden voyage.

What a voyage it must have been—from Maine, down through the Strait of Magellan, and all the way back up to Alaska on the Pacific side. Captain Charles Snape and a crew of eleven managed the entire journey. The ship’s owner, John Borden II, son of the successful dairy producer, joined them from Maine to Bermuda before catching up with them again on the California coast. Roy Chapman Andrews, who later became the director of the museum, was born to be an adventurer. The whale eluded them; however, Andrews captured great footage for future conservation efforts, particularly regarding the depleted populations of fur seals.

Borden sold the vessel in 1914 in San Francisco to the San Francisco Bar Pilots Association. Sand bars on the ocean floor shift with the tides and currents. Arriving large, deep-hulled ships hire a pilot to guide them into port without getting stuck on the bottom. The Adventuress served as a liveaboard home for these pilots. When a large ship arrived, a pilot would take a small dinghy out, climb aboard, and guide the ship around the shifty sands. During World War II, she took on the extra duty of patrolling for the enemy.

In 1952, she was passed on to the Sea Scouts, an organization focused on seamanship and education. Today, this national historic landmark continues to provide adventurers and explorers a way to experience an exciting journey. A nonprofit named Sound Experience creates opportunities for families, teens, and groups to learn hands-on in overnight adventures, presenting seamanship and symbiotic harmony with our natural environment in an unforgettable way.
